Introduction
Financial markets display empirical characteristics that are difficult to capture within the representative-agent framework. Volatility exhibits changing patterns, bubbles recur, and very large fluctuations are sometimes observed; trading volume is substantial, and volatility and volume are correlated. Empirical studies have shown that although news—by definition disseminated simultaneously to all market participants and interpreted in broadly similar ways—produces intraday volatility spikes, news alone cannot explain total observed market volatility.¹ Thus, a large amount of excess volatility remains unexplained by public information alone.
In a parallel research paper, following 44, we attempt to build a bridge between microstructure models and stochastic-volatility models in order to explain why long-term dependence is observed in volatility autocorrelations. Their development links the Mixture of Distributions Hypothesis (MDH), which describes volatility and volume as a bivariate model driven by an underlying stochastic process: information flows. The microstructure architecture is based on agents who possess asymmetric information and interact strategically. It is therefore possible to derive formally the hyperbolic decay of volatility autocorrelations, as for volume autocorrelations.²
Such an analytical framework, however, cannot accurately describe price formation in a dynamic setting, and microsimulations therefore prove to be highly useful tools for extending the results of these analytical models. Microsimulations are computational algorithms that make it possible to formalize information arrivals and strategic interactions among participants and thereby allow the market price to evolve over time. Investor heterogeneity can thus be handled in a fine-grained manner. Market simulations are therefore a natural complement to analytical microstructure models.
Briefly, two strands of the literature depart from the representative-agent hypothesis and introduce heterogeneity. Agents may hold heterogeneous expectations because of bounded rationality or learning capacity, or because they possess asymmetric information sets. 444, among others, develop artificial financial markets in the first class of models using evolutionary algorithms. 44 provide an interesting application of this framework in their study of recurrent currency crises. In the second class of multi-agent simulations, agents are identical in computational ability, and heterogeneity arises solely from their information sets and hence from private information.
By definition, public information is simultaneously available to all market participants. The information-asymmetry hypothesis has proved highly fruitful for understanding market fluctuations, particularly in foreign exchange, a decentralized market in which information on aggregate order flow is unavailable. See Lyons [2002] for a review. Chakrabarti [2000] developed the first market simulation with Bayesian agents under asymmetric information; Boitout and Piatecki [2003] refine his work and apply it to intraday speculative attacks. LeBaron [2003a,b] combines the two approaches in a framework in which agents have memories of different lengths.
Whatever the reasons giving rise to heterogeneity, its existence cannot be ignored. Agents evolve within a complex system in which optimal rationality must necessarily be strategic and adaptive. As LeBaron [2001b] emphasized, the optimal strategy must be assessed relative to the anticipated actions of other agents. The highly nonlinear nature of price dynamics implies that a strategy considered optimal at time t may later become obsolete or suboptimal. No strategy will systematically outperform, a concept that can be viewed in parallel with the efficient-market hypothesis.
One central result of the microsimulation literature is that the fraction of active agents using a particular strategy must evolve over time to reproduce stylized facts commonly observed in real markets. Kirman and Teyssière [2002a,b], Lux and Marchesi [1999, 2000], Chen et al. [2001], and the present paper explicitly model revisions of forecasting methods within the agent population. Agents revise their trading strategies by comparing the profits earned by other market participants, highlighting the importance of social relations in price dynamics.
Sophisticated agents continuously test different trading strategies and adopt those most relevant to a particular market configuration, making direct communication among agents almost unnecessary while generating large swings in selected strategies. Kirman and Teyssière [2002a,b], Lux and Marchesi [1999, 2000], and our extension adopt the simple view that agents use three strategy types: two chartist strategies and one fundamentalist strategy. This simple framework is sufficiently realistic to generate credible price patterns.
The term “noise trading” is frequently used in the microstructure literature, but is arguably inappropriate here. Black [1986] originally proposed it to describe irrational agents who “trade on noise as if it were information.” Technical analysis, however, is the simplest method used by professionals to forecast nonlinearities, capture market trends and reversals, and construct scenarios for the immediate future path of prices. We therefore prefer the less value-laden terms technical analysts or chartists.
In the foreign-exchange market used as our reference, technical analysis unquestionably dominates other short-term forecasting methods. This remains true despite many nonlinear methods with solid theoretical foundations that emerged from the mid-1980s onward, including nearest-neighbor methods, neural networks, and Markov regime-switching models, whose implementation remains demanding. These methods require greater expertise and consequently rarely appear among the standard tools of the average investor.
Is this practice relevant, or can it be justified merely by its self-fulfilling character? Numerous statistical tests of nonlinearity in returns reject the hypothesis of return independence, using both the BDS test (Brock et al. [1996]) and the variance-ratio test (Lo and MacKinlay [1988]), among others. Moreover, a growing body of evidence suggests that the sign of returns is predictable and that this predictability depends on volatility levels (see Christoffersen and Diebold [2002] and LeBaron [1992] for early evidence).³ Such evidence calls for a more thorough analysis of the profitability of technical analysis.
Almost all simulations focus on reproducing stylized facts at a daily horizon, with the notable exception of Chakrabarti [2000]. Few studies therefore attempt to model trading time realistically. Yet trading time is highly irregular and strongly correlated. In this simulation, we extend the Lux–Marchesi framework by allowing trading time to be random. Price observations are consequently unevenly distributed over time, although we sample the data at regular intervals to study their statistical properties at a daily horizon.
Like many studies before us, we focus on long-term dependence in volatility. We also extend similar tests and semiparametric estimators to trading volume, since comparable behavior is commonly documented for both variables. We test for global linear and nonlinear relationships in the simulated price process and ask whether trend-following or contrarian strategies increase the linear or nonlinear predictability of the simulated series. The conclusion is clear: relative to a panel of nine currencies used as a benchmark during calibration, the simulated trajectories remain too predictable.
Finally, we test whether the price samples appear multifractal by applying graphical multiscale analysis. The tests show that long-term dependence in both volatility and volume is well reproduced by the simulation for the selected parameter values, while the scaling functions reveal highly convincing multifractal behavior. Surprisingly, during calibration a trade-off emerges between reproducing fat tails and reproducing long-term dependence in volatility.
The paper is organized as follows. Section 1 briefly describes stylized facts observed for most speculative financial assets, with particular attention to our currency basket. Sections 5 to 7 apply a broad set of statistical tests to twenty simulated samples. Section 5 focuses on distribution tails and nonlinearity tests; Section 6 characterizes autocorrelation functions of volatility and volume; Section 7 considers temporal aggregation of volatility and structure functions. Section 8 compares real and simulated data and concludes with directions for future research.

2.1 Review of Stylized Facts
Fact 1: Existence of a unit root in asset prices. Because the data contain a unit root, the martingale hypothesis cannot be rejected. The market price therefore incorporates information efficiently, and significant linear correlations in price dynamics cannot be detected beyond a few minutes of trading.
Fact 2: Fat tails. Normality is clearly rejected for returns at weekly and daily horizons and at higher frequencies, while the unconditional return distribution exhibits power-law decay, with a slope (tail index) between 2.5 and 5. According to this result, kurtosis may be infinite. This nevertheless rules out stable Lévy laws as a possible generator of real data, since their tail index lies between 0 and 2.
Fact 3: Volatility clustering. This characteristic is ubiquitous in financial data: it is the ARCH effect. Volatility evolves over time, with high-volatility events occurring in clusters. An autoregressive process governs volatility, as in ARCH–GARCH or stochastic-volatility models. However, the residuals of a GARCH model typically remain non-Gaussian, indicating that an autoregressive volatility model alone cannot capture the fat tails of the unconditional distribution.
Fact 4: Long-term dependence in volatility and volume autocorrelations. Absolute or squared returns—possible measures of daily volatility—display long-term dependence in their autocorrelation functions, hence long memory. Serial correlations in volatility can be fitted by a power law over horizons from one day to one year, with an exponent between 0.2 and 0.5. Similar evidence exists for volume.
Fact 5: Strong correlation between volatility and volume. Formal tests generally do not reject a common long-memory signature in the autocorrelation functions of volatility and volume, even though their very-short-run responses to news are not necessarily identical.
Fact 6: Multiscale behavior. Long-term dependence in volatility at different sampling frequencies implies that bursts of volatility cannot be associated with a single time scale. Following Ding, Engle and Granger [1993], absolute returns raised to different powers display similar behavior but different hyperbolic-decay parameters. Müller et al. [1997] document an intriguing information cascade among volatilities at different scales, subsequently confirmed by studies in physics. Similar conclusions have been obtained using both graphical and geometrical multiscale approaches.⁴ Taken together, these characteristics point toward a multifractal signature in financial data.
⁴ See Turiel and Pérez-Vicente [2002] for a rigorous geometrical analysis.
2.2 A Microstructure Model of Financial Data
2.2.1 The Agent Population and Its Strategy Set
In the agent-based modeling literature, agents range from zero-intelligence agents to sophisticated agents based on genetic algorithms. In the Lux–Marchesi framework [1999, 2000], from which our simulation is derived, agents are rational and choose among a predefined set of trading rules according to their respective current profitability. Here they may switch between two chartist strategies and one fundamentalist strategy. Optimistic chartists are naturally buyers, anticipating a rise, while pessimistic chartists behave in the opposite way. Fundamentalists buy or sell the asset depending on whether they believe it is undervalued or overvalued relative to fundamental value. This model is close to the behavior of agents in real markets.
Frankel and Froot [1990], Taylor [1992], and Lui and Mole [1998] document that both chartist and fundamentalist strategies are widely used in foreign-exchange markets and, more importantly, that their relative weights evolve over time. Practitioners broadly report that technical analysis is useful over short horizons, whereas fundamental analysis becomes relevant for forecasting horizons beyond a quarter. Since most active market participants hold positions only over short horizons, chartist analysis is particularly important in foreign exchange.
Goodhart [1988] describes the foreign-exchange market as a random walk with an anchor: fundamental value. In the short run, the market price fluctuates around fundamental value because of both noise traders and uncertainty about that fundamental value. This explains why market sentiment is relevant. A natural proxy is total trading volume, which agents observe to infer the current sentiment of other participants. An indicator of market opinion is therefore included in the chartist strategies in our analysis.
Chartist analysis can be useful for two reasons. First, these strategies provide information about market trends and the aggregate composition of market participants. Second, if systematic inefficiencies are detected, they will be arbitraged away. Finally, because simple chartist strategies appear to outperform other strategies (see Blake et al. [1992]), it is rational for investors to include them in their opportunity set. By construction, chartist strategies are backward-looking and attempt to extract valuable information from price history. Such behavior is relevant in a market where information is incorporated into prices only gradually; past prices may then help forecast future prices.
A growing theoretical literature—behavioral finance—has emerged to explain the consequences of herd behavior in markets (see Orléan [2001] and Schleifer [2000] for a review). It seeks to understand the emergence, persistence, and impact of herd behavior in financial markets. Recent events, including the IT equity bubble and the collapse of financial systems in emerging economies, provide direct fields of investigation.
Strictly speaking, agents in our model differ only through the strategies they have chosen. Even though they reassess their strategies and switch asynchronously from one strategy to another, the degree of heterogeneity is relatively low because there are only three strategy classes. This degree of heterogeneity is not an innocuous issue.
In the Santa Fe artificial market (Arthur et al. [1997]), agents are homogeneous at the beginning of the simulation in terms of learning ability and available strategies. Heterogeneity develops endogenously over time. The Minority Game literature 44 appears able to reproduce most of the stylized facts listed above even though its market structure is weak; here again, trading strategies evolve over time and heterogeneity is broad because agents are not restricted ad hoc to classes. The optimal degree of heterogeneity among agents in microsimulations remains an important open problem because it is directly related to liquidity.
In our simulation, agents may revise their trading strategies following random bilateral encounters with a counterparty, while aggregate order-flow effects on individual decisions are also allowed. Each transition probability is therefore weighted by the size of the counterparty subpopulation. Transition probabilities are drawn from exponential distributions with time-varying parameters. A subtle feature of our architecture is that market-price revisions and news arrivals are modeled according to the same scheme: both are drawn from exponential laws. This assumption makes it possible to describe market activity at each time step as the state of a complex system Y(t), described below.
Because the simulated price series has the martingale property around fundamental value, no strategy is systematically superior to another and no strategy has an expected gain different from zero. This does not exclude nonlinearities in returns. Since no budget constraint is incorporated, an agent can persist in an erroneous trading strategy even as wealth approaches zero. Importantly, there is no evolutionary dynamics in the strict sense and no intertemporal learning. Agents can only learn which strategy is currently dominant in the market; they do not accumulate knowledge about market mechanisms relative to their first trading day. Herd behavior is possible, but learning is not.
2.2.2 State-Transition Probabilities in a Continuous-Time Model
In our setting, aggregate market activity is described by a vector Y(t) in R⁴. The set of possible events is limited. An event is defined as:
1. An agent switching from one strategy to another, with the resulting impact on the order book;
2. The arrival of information about the fundamental value;
3. A revision of the market price by the market maker.
To describe the market dynamics of the simulation completely, we therefore need only describe the vector Y(t):
Y(t) = { n₊(t)/n , n₋(t)/n , p(t) , p_f(t) }
n₊/n, n₋/n ∈ {0, 1/n, 2/n, …, 1−1/n, 1},     p, p_f ∈ (0,+∞)
n₊/n and n₋/n denote respectively the fractions of optimistic and pessimistic chartists in a total population of n agents. The fundamentalist subpopulation is obtained by difference. p and p_f denote the market price and the fundamental value of the exchange rate.
Because these events are Poisson by construction, the process is close to a continuous-time Markov chain, or more simply a Markov process. It is not, however, Markovian in the strict sense. The stochastic process described by the transition probabilities is not memoryless, as one might expect for events drawn from independent exponential distributions. Transition probabilities between optimistic and pessimistic chartists contain a lagged term through the moving-average definition. The process therefore has non-zero but finite memory, bounded by the strategy horizon. Any system of this type in discrete time can be made Markovian through an appropriate change of variables. The continuous-time model is then obtained as a limiting case. See Delahaut [2003] for a rigorous formal treatment.
One point should be emphasized: in continuous time, the probability that several events occur simultaneously is formally zero. The transition-probability dynamics therefore imply asynchronous switching between strategies, even though intervals between switches may be arbitrarily small.
Investors’ trading decisions are not the explicit result of formal maximization of an intertemporal utility function. Nevertheless, as Lux [1997] noted, such transition probabilities can be formally derived from a maximization program for rational agents whose utility functions include social-interaction terms and error terms drawn from an extreme-value distribution. Brock and Durlauf [2000] review this discrete-choice literature.
Modeling the Transition Probabilities
The dynamics of Y(t) are affected by ten possible events: one agent in the population of n agents may switch from one strategy to another, while both the market price and the fundamental price may move up or down by 0.1, the basic increment. We can therefore define the transition vectors lᵢ, i = 1,…,10:
pessimist → optimist:            l₁ = (1/n, −1/n, 0, 0)
optimist → pessimist:            l₂ = (−1/n, 1/n, 0, 0)
fundamentalist → optimist:       l₃ = (1/n, 0, 0, 0)
optimist → fundamentalist:       l₄ = (−1/n, 0, 0, 0)
fundamentalist → pessimist:      l₅ = (0, 1/n, 0, 0)
pessimist → fundamentalist:      l₆ = (0, −1/n, 0, 0)
upward market-price move:        l₇ = (0, 0, 0.1, 0)
downward market-price move:      l₈ = (0, 0, −0.1, 0)
upward fundamental-value move:   l₉ = (0, 0, 0, 0.1)
downward fundamental-value move: l₁₀ = (0, 0, 0, −0.1)
Transition rates are denoted by βᵢ, and the transition probabilities are defined as
P[Y(t+h)=Y(t)+lᵢ | Y(t)=y] = βᵢ(y)h + o(h)
where βᵢ(y), for i ∈ [1,10], is the instantaneous jump probability, also called the jump rate:
βᵢ(y) = limₕ→0⁺  P[Y(t+h)=Y(t)+lᵢ | Y(t)=y] / h
Although the process is not Markovian in the strict sense, its transition probabilities satisfy the usual properties associated with this type of process. Let p_uv(t) denote the probability of transition from state u to state v after duration t:
p_uv(0) = 1 if u=v; 0 if u≠v
limₜ→0⁺ p_uv(t) = 1 if u=v; 0 if u≠v
0 ≤ p_uv ≤ 1,     and     Σᵤ,ᵥ p_uv(t) = 1
β₁(y) = ν₁(n₋/n)(n_c/n)e^(U₁)
β₂(y) = ν₁(n₊/n)(n_c/n)e^(−U₁)
β₃(y) = ν₂(n_f/n)(n₊/n)e^(U₂,₁)
β₄(y) = ν₂(n_f/n)(n₊/n)e^(−U₂,₁)
β₅(y) = ν₂(n_f/n)(n₋/n)e^(U₂,₂)
β₆(y) = ν₂(n₋/n)(n_f/n)e^(−U₂,₂)
β₇(y) = β ED
β₈(y) = −β ED
β₉(y) = ν₃

The following example illustrates how transition rates are constructed when an agent switches from one strategy to another.
Let A be the event “the selected agent is a pessimistic chartist.” Its probability is n₋/n. Let B be the event “the agent meets another chartist.” Its probability is n_c/n. Let C be the event “the agent changes trading strategy.” Its probability is ν₁e^(U₁).
The probability that a pessimistic chartist becomes optimistic is therefore:
P(A ∩ B ∩ C) = P(C | B,A) P(B | A) P(A)
β₁(y) = ν₁(n₋/n)(n_c/n)e^(U₁)
A similar construction yields the other transition rates. Rates β₇ and β₈ reflect the market maker’s relative impatience to revise the price, whereas β₉ and β₁₀ are related to the arrival of information about fundamental value. Information arrivals are modeled as the combination of processes distributed according to exponential distributions with the same constant parameter. This implies that the normality—and hence iid—assumption for unanticipated news is satisfied asymptotically. The following subsections describe the construction of the transition rates in greater detail.
Switching Between Optimism and Pessimism
The force term U₁ in β₁ and β₂ is the weighted sum of two components. The first is an opinion index capturing the effect of majority opinion on chartists and hence measuring herding. The second is a simple momentum strategy based on a moving average.
Formally, the opinion index x is the difference between optimists n₊ and pessimists n₋ divided by the total number of chartists (n_c = n₊ + n₋):
x = (n₊ − n₋)/(n₊ + n₋),     x ∈ [−1,1]
This measure is scale-free and varies over [−1,1]. We then obtain the force term U₁, which includes the factors driving a change of state:
U₁ = α₁x + (α₂/ν₁) · [p(t) − p(t−τ)]/τ
where τ measures the length, in calendar time, of the trend-capture component. It typically reveals the chartists’ time horizon, whether they are trend followers or contrarians.
Switching Between Optimist/Pessimist and Fundamentalist Strategies
Switching from a technical strategy to a fundamentalist strategy is formalized in the same way. The force terms can be defined as differences between the gains from each strategy. U₂,₁ denotes the force term between optimist and fundamentalist, and U₂,₂ the force term between pessimist and fundamentalist:
fundamentalist ↔ pessimist:   U₂,₂ = α₃ { R − [ r + (1/ν₂)(p−p(t−τ))/τ ]/p − s |(p_f−p)/p| }
fundamentalist ↔ optimist:    U₂,₁ = α₃ { [ r + (1/ν₂)(p−p(t−τ))/τ ]/p − R − s |(p_f−p)/p| }
In the first expression, pessimists and chartists leave the market and therefore earn the domestic risk-free rate R, while losing the foreign rate r and the market return over the period. Fundamentalists expect to earn the difference between fundamental value and market price. Because the gain from this arbitrage is not immediate, a discount rate s is added.

2.2.4 Trading Mechanism and Pricing Rule
The trading mechanism is relatively elementary. Both technical analysts and fundamentalists continuously submit orders to the order book. They post orders without transaction costs to a market maker, who adjusts the price when necessary. This pricing rule is not innocuous, because liquidity is thereby always guaranteed. Total excess demand is the sum of demand from each class:
ED_c = (n₊ − n₋) t_c
ED_f = n_f γ ((p_f − p)/p)
ED = (n₊ − n₋) t_c + n_f γ ((p_f − p)/p)
Order flow is an aggregate variable and, by definition, corresponds to signed trading volume (positive or negative); it is a variant of total excess demand. The main difference is that order flow measures actual transactions, whereas a change in demand does not necessarily imply a transaction.
The pricing rule is relatively simple and is also common in the literature. Positive excess demand raises the price, while negative excess demand lowers it. Each transaction is executed without necessarily requiring an opposite order because the fictitious market maker acts as the systematic counterparty to every transaction. We neglect the inventory-management issues implied by this position; the market maker can therefore be regarded as fictitious. No bid–ask spread is included in the price.
The microstructure literature explains the existence of the spread through inventory costs and information asymmetry. Some microsimulations (Jefferies et al. [2000]) use an ad hoc rule to incorporate a time-varying spread. This rule makes the spread negatively correlated with trading volume: the stronger current excess demand is—equal by construction to volume—the smaller the spread.
In a different setting, Chakrabarti [2000] develops a market scheme in which the bid–ask quote is derived from an optimization program under the assumption that the market maker is risk-averse. Our architecture is clearly simpler. The market maker’s role is incorporated through a probability of market-price revision that increases exponentially with excess demand. The market price is adjusted once the magnitude of order flow exceeds a desired inventory level. Formally:
P(P(tᵢ₊₁) − P(tᵢ) | ED) = exp(βED),     if ED > 0
P(P(tᵢ₊₁) − P(tᵢ) | ED) = exp(−βED),    if ED < 0
The price is adjusted whenever a “market-price jump” event has the minimum waiting time in the event vector. The rule is:
P(tᵢ₊₁) − P(tᵢ) = +0.1,    if ED > 0
P(tᵢ₊₁) − P(tᵢ) = −0.1,    if ED < 0
This implies a tick size of 0.1.⁵
2.2.5 Random Trading Time
In this simulation, all possible events occur asynchronously and irregularly, forming clusters of events during turbulent episodes. Like any other event, the market price is revised irregularly by the market maker. As in real markets, the duration between new quotes is irregular. To our knowledge, no other simulation takes this point into account, because most seek to reproduce stylized facts at a daily horizon. The issue of temporality is therefore almost absent from the literature, even though it is closely related to liquidity. This is not an innocuous issue. Recall that Lux and Marchesi [1999, 2000] and Chen et al. [2001] must adjust the length of time increments arbitrarily in order to obtain realistic results.
An autoregressive conditional duration (ACD) model captures the irregular nature of durations. Engle and Russell [1998] initiated this literature. The ACD model specifies observed duration as a mixed process combining an autoregressive model for expected conditional durations with iid residuals following a Weibull distribution (1,γ). For γ = 1, the Weibull distribution becomes an exponential distribution. The real-time properties of the simulation therefore open the way to future research on its robustness using real-time data.
